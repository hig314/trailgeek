Why the answer is known

The engine prices a line in equivalent metres: its length along the slope, plus for each metre a charge from each per-metre factor (its weight times its curve), plus a charge for each corner and bend. On these smooth surfaces every per-metre factor depends only on the line's grade g and the ground's slope s: grade itself; the angle to the fall line, whose cosine is g / s; fall-line tread inside the fall-line band; side slope; cut and fill at its floor. So a metre along the slope costs m(g, s), and climbing a metre costs

c(g, s) = m(g, s) √(1 + g²) / g

A climb that never descends gains each metre of height once, and can climb no steeper than the ground, so when turning is free the cheapest line climbs each metre at the cheapest grade the ground allows (held under a cap, because the steepest 10 m is charged again). On ground steeper than that grade it crosses the contours at a fixed angle, a logarithmic spiral on a cone; on gentler ground it goes straight up the fall line.

That is exact only while turns cost nothing. The formula's line still pays the bend weight for its curvature and the corner weight for any corner, so the engine may beat it by turning less. On the uniform slope the number of switchbacks is a trade the formula cannot settle alone; there the reference line is the best zigzag at the best grade over every switchback count and radius, scored by the engine.

The Graduation Peak gully is real ground, from lidar, where no formula holds: the slope changes from metre to metre and the gullies' walls hem the line in. It shows the same engine, with the same weights, choosing a line where the answer is not known. Its start and end are made up.

How the in-between lines were found

Each setting on the grid was run thoroughly: two seeds, long searches, then searched again from each neighbouring setting's line, so a better route found next door is not missed. Between two neighbouring settings, the line was traced in ten small steps from each end: each step searches only a narrow strip around the step before's line, leaning toward the other end's line. At each step the cheaper of the two tracings is shown. Where the two ends are versions of one route, the line slides from one to the other; where they are different routes, it jumps where their costs cross.

Dig-in

The design line can sit below the ground (cut) or above it (fill), within a limit along the line and a deeper one where a corner is dug into the slope. The ticks across the line show where, their length eight times the depth. With the cut and fill weight low, a corner dug in can hide a dip the trail walks on the ground: the design line climbs steadily while the ground under it falls and rises.
